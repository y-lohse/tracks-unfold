# Voicing sculpture

## Role

Arrange the notes of supplied chords to fit a spatial problem while preserving their identities. Begin with one chord; later connect several chord arrangements through individual voice movements.

**Voice paths is merged into this type**, not a separate puzzle or unlock. Single-chord placement is the conceptual foundation; movement between chords develops the same arrangement problem across time.

The foundation is retained for further design. This is not a behavioral specification: the board, interaction, generator and assessment remain open. Use the shared [[Run director]] when those details are developed.

## Player action

Move notes between registers, reorder them and, when allowed, double them so the arrangement fits the puzzle's requirements. Possible requirements include a fixed bass, a fixed top note, available register regions, a voice count or locked notes.

In multi-chord forms, choose later arrangements that also satisfy movement requirements between persistent voices: a maximum move, a retained common tone, a supplied bass line or a particular tendency-tone resolution. Introduce these separately rather than stacking every requirement into each puzzle.

The intended loop is to inspect the space, rearrange the notes, audition the result and submit a fitting arrangement or sequence. Audition and submission details are proposals, not settled rules. Selecting notes one at a time should be sufficient; simultaneous keyboard performance is not required.

A keyboard may support auditioning, but movable note pieces or register lanes may make the puzzle clearer. The multi-chord form should extend that interaction rather than require a large score editor.

## Single-chord example

The starting C-major chord is C3–E3–G3. The destination has three voices, E3 locked as its lowest note, and no room above C4. Preserve all three chord tones without doubling.

E3–G3–C4 fits. The player has put the chord in first inversion by solving the placement problem. “First inversion” names the relationship they used; it need not be the opening instruction.

This is a small introductory example, not a claim that these particular restrictions sustain a whole type. Later puzzles need meaningful choices about register, doubling and fixed notes rather than simply asking for a named inversion repeatedly.

## Multi-chord example

Start with C4–E4–G4. The next chords must be A minor and G major. Keep three voices, include all three tones of each chord and move each voice by no more than two semitones at each change.

One valid sequence is:

| Voice | C major | A minor | G major |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lower | C4 | C4 | B3 |
| Middle | E4 | E4 | D4 |
| Upper | G4 | A4 | G4 |

The chords must fit both their local placement requirements and their neighbors. A locally valid arrangement may require too much movement to reach the next chord, creating a reason to consider more than one step.

The chord identities are supplied. Choosing which chords should occur is a different demand, as in [[Harmony fitting]]; it is not silently added to this type's opening forms.

## Sources of variation

- Chord identity, register and available pitch regions.
- Fixed bass or top note and other locked pitches.
- Number of voices and permitted doubling.
- Compact versus spread arrangements under explicit range requirements.
- Slash basses, including a bass outside the named chord.
- Seventh chords and third inversion after the relevant foundations.
- Number of chord transitions and initial voicing.
- Movement limits, retained common tones and supplied bass lines.
- Focused dominant-seventh resolution questions.

A focused resolution form could ask the player to route B to C and F down to E in G7 → C. Other voices may be supplied so this need not begin as a complete four-voice arranging exercise.

Use combinations that leave a real placement decision. More notes, more transitions or more restrictive regions are not automatically more interesting.

## Answer boundaries

- Preserve each required chord's tones, subject to the stated doubling and bass rules.
- The lowest sounding pitch determines inversion; moving upper notes need not change it.
- Distinguish root from bass and exact pitch from pitch-class identity.
- A non-chord-tone slash bass is an explicit addition to the bass requirement, not an inversion or an accidental error.
- In multi-chord forms, voice identity persists across transitions so movement is checkable. Rules about crossings must be explicit if used.
- Accept every arrangement or sequence satisfying the visible requirements, not only the shortest or the generator's preferred solution.
- Use specific movement bounds or retained notes, not an opaque “smoothness” score. Do not import unstated counterpoint rules.
- A taught tendency-tone resolution is the contract for that question, not a claim that all other resolutions are invalid music.
- Generate and validate a complete solution before presenting the puzzle. In multi-chord forms, individually feasible chords are not enough; the complete sequence must connect legally.

## Learning coverage and readiness

Single-chord forms can contribute to:

- [[C4 - Triad inversions]]: recognizing and constructing bass-defined inversions.
- [[C5 - Voicing and doubling]]: rearranging, spreading and doubling notes while preserving identity.
- [[C6 - Slash chords and specified bass]]: realizing chord-tone and non-chord-tone bass requirements.
- [[C8 - Seventh-chord inversions]]: later arrangements including the seventh in the bass.
- [[P2 - Octaves and note identity]] through register changes.

Start from relevant [[C1 - Major and minor triads]] familiarity. Voicing/doubling adds C4/P2; slash bass adds C4; seventh-inversion forms add C7/C5. Do not require the complete chord vocabulary to begin.

Multi-chord forms can contribute to:

- [[H7 - Connecting chord voices]]: common tones, small movements and deliberate inversion/voicing choices, after relevant C5/H2 familiarity.
- [[H6 - Dominant seventh resolution]]: tracking the leading tone and chordal seventh in a resolving progression. The focused form needs C7/H4, not completion of all H7 work.

Supplying all chord tones supports placement but does not establish independent chord construction. Recognition of what changed—voicing, inversion or identity—also needs deliberate evidence. Selecting V7–I without determining the marked voices' movements does not demonstrate full H6; supplying the chord sequence does not establish independent harmonic-function choice.

The merged type provides a use for chord knowledge, not by itself a complete way of initially teaching chord construction. Separate acquisition-game proposals address that need.

## Open design work

- Find a board and a small set of placement rules that make rearrangement satisfying rather than a dressed-up inversion quiz.
- Develop multi-chord movement as an extension of that board, keeping single-chord forms independently accessible.
- Choose how notes move, which information is supplied, and how the resulting sound supports the interaction.
- Test variation depth with a few triads and short sequences before relying on richer chord vocabulary.
- Define question forms, puzzle-type skills and assessment only after the central loop is clearer.
