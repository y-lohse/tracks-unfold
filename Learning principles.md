# Learning principles

## Purpose and how to use this document

Tracks Unfold aims to develop musical understanding, memory and fluency through intrinsically enjoyable, replayable puzzles and minigames. The player plays a game; the musical relationships they repeatedly use, hear and notice are the substance of the learning.

These are guiding principles, not a compliance checklist. They express the direction to favor when making trade-offs, not a requirement that every puzzle embody every principle equally. The first three capture the project's core priorities; the complementary guidance develops their implications and patterns already present in the puzzle concepts. Refine it through design and playtesting.

This document concerns learning and puzzle design. It does not redesign the meta-game, run director, proficiency estimates or unlock rules.

## Core priorities

### 1. Understanding through practice and discovery

There are no separate theory lessons followed by practice exercises. Players learn through doing: trying moves, observing consequences, hearing differences, comparing examples and discovering relationships. Design for those observations and deductions rather than assuming that exposure alone will make them happen.

The immediate objective is the game's objective: clear the board, deliver the parcels, fit the notes, reproduce the phrase. Learning is the designer's purpose, not an extra task the player must perform after solving the puzzle. A successful move need not be followed by an explanation, a theory question or proof that the player understood it.

Rules, labels, references, corrections and occasional contextual tips can help players participate and make sense of what they encounter. They are not a lesson sequence or a requirement to explain every relevant fact before play. Often a name can attach to a relationship the player has already used. When a design document states a musical fact, read it first as guidance for the designer—not automatically as required player-facing copy.

For example, fitting E–G–C into a space with E in the bass lets the player use a first inversion. The game can make the unchanged chord identity and changed bass noticeable without opening with a definition of inversion.

### 2. Memory and fluency through varied repetition

Understanding a relationship once, remembering its applications and using it naturally are different stages of familiarity. Repeated play should help musical knowledge become readily available, not remain a rule the player laboriously reconstructs every time. Memorization is a desired outcome, not a failure of understanding.

Build games worth returning to. Repeat the underlying relationships while varying the situations in which they are useful: roots, keys, registers, sounds, layouts, goals and strategic choices, as appropriate to the puzzle. Familiar examples can reinforce memory; unfamiliar combinations can broaden it. The aim is not novelty on every move, nor endless copies of one exercise.

Variation should sustain both musical practice and interesting play. Merely transposing the same solved board may offer useful note practice, but may not keep the puzzle interesting. Conversely, ever more elaborate strategy can crowd out the musical repetition the game exists to provide.

The ambition is durable understanding and fluent recall over repeated visits, not a promise that any fixed number of plays produces perfect mastery. Introduce enough challenge to invite attention without treating speed, missing support or added complexity as inherently better learning.

### 3. Keyboard familiarity as a supported secondary objective

The primary objective is musical understanding and fluency. Where it helps, use a recognizable keyboard to connect note names, pitch distances, chord contents and voicings to locations a player could use on an instrument. Knowing the notes on a keyboard and understanding which keys form a chord are worthwhile outcomes.

A phone app is not a substitute for practicing a full keyboard, and keyboard technique is not the product's claim. Do not require realistic hand spans, fingering, simultaneous key presses or an instrument-sized interface just to preserve a keyboard metaphor.

Favor keyboard familiarity when it fits the interaction; choose tiles, lanes, diagrams or another representation when they make the musical relationship or the game clearer. Keyboard transfer is a benefit to encourage, not a constraint that overrides the learning or portrait-phone usability.

## Complementary design guidance

### Make musical skills a means to the game's end

Ideally, the skill being developed is a tool for meeting an engaging challenge, not the challenge's ultimate purpose. The player wants to clear a board, route objects or solve an arrangement problem; musical knowledge helps them work out which actions are possible and how to use them. Repeatedly using those means creates opportunities for practice, learning and understanding.

Better understanding should make the player more proficient with those tools: noticing available moves, anticipating their consequences and using them fluently. That can lead to better game outcomes, but indirectly, through play and decisions—not because displaying more theoretical knowledge is itself a better outcome. Knowing every legal move does not necessarily reveal which move best serves the goal. The challenge should ideally remain interesting even when the musical tools are familiar.

For example, chord contents might determine which tiles can be removed, while the board makes choosing between valid removals interesting. Scale membership might determine which objects move and where, while their destinations make the order of operations matter. Musical correctness makes an action available; using it well serves the game's objective.

This is a direction, not a ban on direct recognition, imitation or practice games. A satisfying listening or performance loop can be enough; not every game needs a separate strategic layer. Favor designs where musical fluency empowers play rather than becoming the end in itself. The musical activity should belong to the challenge, not be an admission fee before an unrelated reward.

### Support is part of learning, not a defect in it

References, highlighted relationships, previews, playback and guided constructions can let a beginner participate, compare and notice patterns. A player can learn from information supplied by the game even when their answer does not independently demonstrate that knowledge.

Keep support useful to the current experience. It can become lighter as familiarity grows, but removing help is not an objective in itself. Preserve opportunities for meaningful choices and musical attention rather than either supplying every move forever or withholding help to obtain cleaner measurements.

### Separate learning opportunities from claims about proficiency

The curriculum describes musical capabilities worth developing. It is not an assessment specification, a list of mandatory in-app demonstrations or a demand to prove that every learning outcome has occurred. Those capabilities can be useful beyond the app and need not all be directly observable within it.

“Does this help the player learn?” and “What can this response tell us about proficiency?” are different questions. The second matters when designing an actual proficiency update; it is not a universal objection to a learning mechanic. Keep measurement claims proportionate without making measurement the purpose of play.

For example, hearing a chord while its name is visible offers a useful sound–name association. It does not establish independent identification by ear, but that is not a reason to remove the label, suppress the sound or add a listening test after every move. Conversely, recognizing a written chord name and recognizing its sound remain different abilities; a productive learning opportunity does not justify claiming they are interchangeable.

Existing assessment mechanics retain their own contracts. These principles neither change their rules nor turn their evidence requirements into requirements for every learning interaction.

### Make consequences understandable and exploration worthwhile

Help the player perceive what a move changes and what it preserves, through relevant sound, visual relationships and feedback. Comparison can make a pattern discoverable without a verbal explanation. Feedback should help the next attempt, not merely announce success or failure.

Distinguish musical correctness from strategic usefulness. A valid chord can be a poor board-clearing choice; a legal voicing can leave no useful next move. Where the puzzle permits it, auditioning, rearranging and trying alternatives can be part of the learning. This is guidance for interaction design, not a change to submission or mistake rules.

### Keep the music accurate without making the game encyclopedic

Use clear musical contexts and understandable puzzle requirements. A puzzle's restrictions are not universal laws of good music. Accept alternatives that satisfy its requirements rather than privileging the generator's answer or an unstated aesthetic preference.

Simplify the vocabulary and isolate useful relationships without falsifying them. There is no obligation to explain every exception, historical distinction or advanced application before a player can enjoy a basic example. Naming, hearing, construction and interpretation can reinforce one another across different games; each puzzle need not do all of them at once.

## Reading the curriculum and puzzle documents

- [Curriculum — skill map](Curriculum/Curriculum%20-%20Skill%20map.md) describes the musical capabilities, their foundations and current scope. “What this skill enables” describes what someone familiar with the skill can do, in or beyond the app. It does not prescribe a quiz, explanation screen or mastery gate.
- [Curriculum — practical outcomes](Curriculum/Curriculum%20-%20Practical%20outcomes.md) describes how those capabilities combine in musical use. These are destinations for learning, not final exams or promised instrument-performance achievements.
- The documents in `Puzzle Types/` explore or specify ways to make those relationships playable. Respect their stated maturity: a provisional concept still needs design and playtesting. Read facts and learning-coverage notes through these principles, while preserving explicit behavioral contracts until deliberately revised.

When reviewing a design, ask what the player is trying to achieve, how musical skills help them pursue that goal, what they repeatedly do and can notice, why they would want to play again and where support helps. If the concern is instead about an assessment claim, identify that specific claim rather than treating a lack of independent proof as a lack of learning value.
